\section{LaSalle's invariance principle}

Lyapunov's theorems conclude stability from a strict decrease of $V$. LaSalle's principle covers
the case $\dot{V} \le 0$, where $V$ may stall: a trajectory confined to a compact set must then
settle into the region where $\dot{V}$ vanishes. The mechanism is the $\omega$-limit set. Along a
trajectory $V$ is nonincreasing and bounded below, hence convergent, so $V$ is \emph{constant} on
$\omega(\varphi)$ --- and a set on which a solution can live while $V$ stays constant is one where
$\dot{V} = 0$. Making that last step a theorem rather than a picture is what the invariance of
$\omega(\varphi)$ supplies, and it is the only place uniqueness of solutions is needed.

\section{Uniform decay envelopes}

A uniform convergence-time hypothesis says that for each tolerance $\eta$ there is a delay
$T(\eta)$ after which every trajectory is inside the $\eta$-ball. Read the other way round, it
bounds how far a trajectory can still be after a given elapsed time — but only through a
function of $\eta$ that may be discontinuous, and so is not yet a comparison function.

The two results below do the regularization: they turn the delay into a genuine decay envelope,
a function of elapsed time alone that is continuous, positive and vanishing at $+\infty$. That
envelope is the time-decay half of the class $\mathcal{KL}$ bounds of the next section; the
class $\mathcal{K}$ bound supplies the other half, the dependence on the initial deviation.

\section{Inverses of antitone functions}

A function $W$ that is continuous and strictly antitone on $(0,\infty)$, blows up at $0^+$ and
vanishes at $+\infty$ surjects onto $(0,\infty)$, and its right inverse has the same shape.  This
is the mechanism behind the class $\mathcal{KL}$ construction of the next section: $W$ is a
mean-value bound and its inverse supplies the decay rate.
